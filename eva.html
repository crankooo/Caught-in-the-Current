Helene's smallest sufferers: The river dwellers left behind

By Eva Ciolek Passeri

Some of the storm's most vulnerable victims — river dwellers — suffered dramatically when raging waters washed them downstream. Conservationists say it will take years to recover the damage.

Cover Photo: Eva Ciolek Passeri

The hellbender salamander, North America's largest aquatic salamander, lives among rocks in fast-moving streams — putting it directly in Helene's path.

Hans Lohmeyer is standing in a lively park on the outskirts of Asheville as children and dogs gambol around him. He's just about to step onto a nature trail along Cane Creek that was only recently reopened after Tropical Storm Helene ravaged its banks and many of its inhabitants.

"It experienced just crazy amounts of tree loss and damage. You'll see it along the creek…the flooding damage, destabilization from the stream banks," Lohmeyer said as he walked along the now placid waterway at Bill Moore Community Park in Fletcher, North Carolina. "I don't think it'll be quite the same as what it was before, just due to the sheer power and the impact from the [storm]."

Lohmeyer is stewardship manager for Conserving Carolina, a nonprofit that works to preserve land and water resources throughout Western North Carolina by focusing on creating trails and educating the community about the importance of habitat. What he's particularly interested in these days is one of the smallest creatures that suffered significantly from Helene: freshwater mussels.

First, these tiny mollusks experienced the rushing water as it swelled the rivers and creeks where they live. Second, they had to endure a cleanup that involved massive construction vehicles driving up and down to scrape out riverbeds and remove thousands of pounds of washed-out debris.

"A lot of mussels got run over," Lohmeyer said about the vulnerable freshwater population that resides in Western North Carolina. These small creatures, some on endangered species lists, cluster together in "beds" among rocks and eddies. They are critical to the ecosystem because they serve as nature's filtering system, siphoning out pollutants as water runs over their extended necks. "The storm did have a very negative impact on those populations."

The fallout for this largely unseen and unknown creature is just one example of the extent of damage the storm wrought in the natural world alongside the better-publicized human one. Mussels are just one species that scientists and researchers are watching and worrying about. Another to take a hard hit was the famously ugly hellbender salamander, which can grow up to 2 feet long and live up to 30 years.

A bee pollinates a wildflower along the banks of Cane Creek — part of the delicate ecosystem that Helene disrupted.

A double whammy for river life

Freshwater mussels cluster in beds along creek bottoms throughout Western North Carolina. Some species are on the endangered list.

The brownish, yellowish aquatic animal lives in the mud and among the rocks of riverbeds. This, again, put it in the direct line of destruction. The result will likely be felt for generations of hellbenders to come, experts say.

"Those impacts that we're dealing with are probably going to still deal with for the next, at least 10, 15 years," said Hannah Woodburn, a riverkeeper for MountainBlue, a nonprofit that works to protect clean water and resilient forests across the southern Blue Ridge region.

"No. 1, the banks were already pretty eroded from the storm event itself, and then No. 2, that secondary impact of having heavy machinery coming in, accessing, cutting all the live trees and remaining vegetation along from the banks — but then also drove these giant machines up and down the streams as well," she said.

"It was kind of this double whammy. They probably smushed anything that had survived the flood."
